Turbine Alarm Explainer

A Time-Series Language Model that reads the last 24 hours of a turbine's SCADA channels and tells the operations engineer, hours before the controller trips: is a fault stop coming, in which subsystem, and why.

1 · Target user

The wind-farm operations engineer at an asset owner or an independent service provider, monitoring 10–100 turbines from a control room. Their morning starts with the overnight event list: a farm of 14 turbines produces roughly 4,000 status events a year, most of them benign (low wind, battery tests, comms blips), some of them the first sign of a forced outage that costs days of production and a site visit.

Today they answer three questions by hand, scrolling 10-minute plots of 300 channels per turbine:

  • What happened here, and which subsystem is it? (triage the alarm)
  • Does it matter — how much did it cost, does it need a visit? (prioritise)
  • Is this turbine drifting toward a stop? (anticipate)

Secondary users: the site technician who gets the work order, and the asset manager who reviews lost-production reports. This is Aionic's industrials and energy lane in one asset class.

2 · Signals we have

Two native streams per turbine, exported from Greenbyte SCADA by the operator, plus static farm metadata. Everything below has been checked in the raw files.

Stream A — 10-minute SCADA telemetry (the time series)

299 columns are exported; only ~70 are actually populated (the rest are empty channels for this turbine type). The model input is a curated set of ~16 base signals, chosen because they are populated, physically meaningful, and cover the subsystems the alarms talk about:

Stream B — Status / alarm log (the language)

One row per event: Timestamp start, Timestamp end, Duration, Status, Code, Message, Comment, Service contract category, IEC category. Around 120 distinct (status, message) pairs per farm-year. Examples of real messages: Overload generator fan 2 · Brake accumulator defect · Low gearbox oil pressure · Frequency converter error · Grid loss · Tower oscillation X level 2 · Pitch measuring system 1><2 · Brake pads worn · Cable autounwind · Comm. failure FPM · Wind < start wind · Manual stop – on site. The IEC category (Full Performance, Forced outage, Scheduled Maintenance, Technical Standby, Out of Environmental Specification…) tells us whether an event was a real failure.

Static metadata

Turbine type (MM92 vs MM82), rated power (2.05 MW), rotor diameter, hub height, coordinates. This goes into the text context so the model knows which machine it is looking at.

Data facts that shape the design

2016 is incomplete (Kelmarsh temperatures / RPM / pitch only from May 2016) → we use 2017 → 2021 for both farms. 649 of 2,122 status rows in one turbine-year have an open end ("-") → treat as point events. "Battery test" and "Manual stop" dominate the log → excluded from the fault classes, kept as context. After filtering (2017–21): 10,410 fault-type Stop/Warning events at Penmanshiel (2,503 IEC "Forced outage") and 3,387 at Kelmarsh (694); 122 and 87 distinct messages.

3 · Problems we solve, with the farm's own numbers

The log is written at second precision when the fault has already happened. Many fault stops are preceded by hours of visible drift in the signals; today nobody watches 300 channels × 14 turbines for it. The value of the system is lead time: a warning while the turbine is still producing, with the subsystem and the evidence, so the operator can de-rate or schedule instead of taking a forced stop. ≈ 1,260 MWh per year lost to fault-class stops on this one farm (≈ £60–100k/yr at UK wind prices, before call-out costs); ~39 forced outages per turbine per year. Losses are a long tail — the median fault stop is 12 min, the top 5 % last over 6 h — and those long stops are where lead time turns a forced outage into a planned intervention.

  • Early warning. "Fault stop likely on turbine 7 within 6 h" while it is still producing — the log cannot give this.
  • Which subsystem and why, from the signals, so the warning is actionable: de-rate, inspect fan, schedule an oil check.
  • Knowing when to stay quiet. Most windows are normal; a system that cries wolf gets switched off. False-alarm rate is a first-class metric.
  • Working on the next site. Trained on Penmanshiel (MM82), tested on Kelmarsh (MM92) with no re-engineering.
Honest scope

Lead time is expected for the thermal / lubrication / structural / pitch classes and essentially none for grid loss, comms or manual stops. Results are reported per class, never averaged over the unpredictable ones.

4 · Workflow, in Aionic's four stages

Sense & encode

The system takes a 24 h window of the ~16 SCADA channels for one turbine (144 steps at 10 min), the turbine's static description, and the question. Each channel is encoded separately and described in text with its mean, std and unit — the OpenTSLM multi-series "stacking".

Reason & explain

The TSLM writes a short structured finding: which signals changed and how, what the turbine state was (producing / idle / feathered), the most plausible subsystem, and the alarm-level description — before it commits to a label.

Act & execute

One recommended action with the reason: inspect generator fan 1 before the wind returns, no action — low-wind standby, schedule hydraulic check. The engineer approves or dismisses; nothing is executed automatically.

System implications

The finding carries the price: lost production so far (kWh), whether wind is available now, and whether the same message recurred on this turbine recently — the inputs to a prioritised work list across the farm.

Every sentence above is derivable from the window plus the log row. That is the standard for the training targets: no claims the data cannot support. For the early-warning task (T1) the same event is used with the window ending 1, 3 and 6 h earlier, and the answer is Answer: yes, generator_cooling.

In one sentence

T1 is what we sell and what we score first. Its binary form (yes/no) is the MVP and the number on the slide; the subsystem and the evidence text are the language layer added on top of the same records once the binary works. Input = 24 h of 17 SCADA channels ending now + turbine context + the question; output = whether a fault stop is coming in the next H hours, which subsystem, and the evidence.

6 · Input specification

      |<──────── input: 24 h, 144 steps @ 10 min ────────>|<── H ──>|
  ────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┼─────────┼──────
   t-24h                                                  t        alarm
                                                          ^ anchor: nothing after this is seen

Time-series part

  • Window: 24 h = 144 steps at 10 min, ending at the anchor t. 12 h is the fallback, 48 h a later experiment; the connector takes the length as a parameter.
  • Channels: the 17 base signals in section 2, fixed order, one TimeSeries each.
  • Normalisation: per-channel z-score inside the window; original mean / std / unit written into the channel's text description (OpenTSLM convention).
  • Missing values: forward-fill up to 3 steps, else 0 after z-scoring; windows with > 20 % missing in any core channel are dropped.

Text part

  • Context: turbine id and type, rated power, month, state at t (producing / idle), the horizon H, and the number of fault-class events on this turbine in the previous 30 days.
  • Per-channel description: "generator bearing rear temperature in °C, 10-minute means over 24 h, mean 45.0 std 3.6:".
  • Question: the T1–T5 prompt, with the subsystem list for T1 / T3.
  • Never given: anything from after t — no alarm message, code or category.

Anchors and sampling (T1)

  • Positives: for every fault-class Forced outage (repeats of the same message within 2 h deduped to the first), three records with t = alarm − 1 h, − 3 h, − 6 h. Drop a record if the turbine is not producing at t (already stopped → trivial) or if a manual / maintenance stop overlaps the window.
  • Negatives: random anchors where the turbine is producing at t, no fault-class event starts in (t, t + 6 h], and none is ongoing. Sampled 2:1 against positives.
  • Labels stored as annotations: fault_within_1h, fault_within_3h, fault_within_6h (class or none), lead_time_min, next_event_message, next_event_duration_h, next_event_lost_kwh, state_at_anchor.
  • Split (no leakage) — train on the larger farm, hold out the smaller one: train = Penmanshiel turbines 1–12 (no #3 exists), 2017 → 2019; validation = Penmanshiel turbines 13–15, same years (unseen turbines); test A = Penmanshiel, all 14 turbines, 2020 → 2021 (unseen time); test B = Kelmarsh, all 6 turbines, 2017 → 2021 (unseen site and turbine model, MM82 → MM92). Penmanshiel has ~2× the data — 10,410 fault-type events vs 3,387 — so it trains. 73 messages occur on both farms; the 14 Kelmarsh-only ones are rare (≤ 4 each), so the taxonomy is built from the union.

7 · Output specification

The prompt is fixed text (input tokens, no loss); the answer is what the model learns (loss only there). Template for T1:

pre_prompt:
  You are monitoring wind turbine {turbine_id} ({turbine_type}, {rated_kw} kW) in {month}.
  Below are the last 24 hours of 10-minute SCADA signals, ending now. The turbine is
  currently {state}. Analyse the signals and decide whether a fault-related stop
  (forced outage) is likely to begin within the next {H} hours. If yes, name the
  subsystem from: generator_cooling, gearbox_lubrication, pitch_system, brake_hydraulics,
  converter_grid, structural_overspeed, sensor_comms.
  Do not state a decision until the final line. End with "Answer: ".

time_series_text[i]:  "{channel} in {unit}, 10-minute means over 24 h, mean {m} std {s}:"  + 144 values
post_prompt:          Assessment:

Answer, MVP (label only — day 1):

Answer: no
Answer: yes, generator_cooling

Answer, full (evidence first, then label — day 2):

Wind rose from 4 to 8 m/s over the last 6 hours and power ramped from 200 kW to 1.1 MW.
Generator rear bearing temperature climbed 7 °C in 4 hours and is at its 24 h maximum,
while the front bearing stayed flat at 41 °C — the rear side is not being cooled as load
increases. Gear oil and pitch behave normally. This pattern precedes a generator cooling
stop. Recommended: limit power to 1 MW and check generator fan 1.

Production is steady at 6–7 m/s, all temperatures track load, pressures and grid values
are stable. No sign of a developing fault.
Answer: no

The text after Answer: must be exactly parseable — that is what gets scored. The evidence comes before Answer: so the model reasons first (OpenTSLM shows this improves label accuracy). T3 (post-hoc) keeps the five-line FINDING / EVIDENCE / CAUSE / IMPACT / ACTION template of the worked example, followed by Answer: <subsystem>.

How training targets are produced (there are no technician reports in the data):

  1. The Answer: line, kWh, duration and lead time come directly from the log and the Lost Production columns.
  2. Evidence sentences are generated by rules over the window: largest z-score excursions, temperature deltas over the last 3–6 h and vs ambient, front-vs-rear bearing asymmetry, power-vs-wind residual (farm's own power curve), pitch / RPM state, pressure drift, grid steps. Only rule-verified facts go in; nothing from after t.
  3. A frontier LLM paraphrases the rule output into fluent text (OpenTSLM's HAR-CoT recipe), instructed not to add facts. A random 5 % sample is human-checked.
  4. Negatives get short fixed answers.

10 · Not in scope, and limitations we will state

  • No remaining-useful-life or long-horizon forecasting. 10-minute data over 6 years is not enough run-to-failure history per component. (TimesFM-3 can appear as an optional power-forecast baseline, nothing more.)
  • Ground truth is the alarm log, not a technician's diagnosis. The subsystem label is what the controller reported; the true root cause may differ. We say so.
  • Many alarms have no precursor at 10-minute resolution (grid loss, comms). T4 will work for thermal and hydraulic classes and not for others; we report per class rather than hide it in an average.
  • Generated explanations are rule-derived text paraphrased by an LLM. They are faithful to the window by construction, but they are not expert-written.
  • Not every fault has a precursor. Grid loss, comms and manual stops are unpredictable from turbine signals by construction; the headline metric is reported per class, not averaged over them.
  • One manufacturer. Both farms are Senvion; generalisation to Vestas / Siemens is untested.

11 · Decisions for the team

  • Split direction — decided: train on Penmanshiel (larger), hold out Kelmarsh. Verified in the raw files: both farms populate the same core channels and share 73 alarm messages; Kelmarsh-only messages are rare.
  • Window length — 24 h is the proposal (144 steps). 12 h halves the compute; 48 h helps thermal drifts. Decide with the model person.
  • Channel set — the 16 in section 2 or a smaller 8 (wind, power, rotor rpm, pitch A, gen bearing rear, stator, gear oil temp, gear oil pressure)? Fewer channels = faster SoftPrompt; Flamingo does not care much.
  • Lead times — 1 / 3 / 6 h proposed (three records per alarm); 12 h is the ambitious add-on.
  • Negative sampling ratio — 2:1 proposed.
  • T2 / T4 / T5 — only if T1 is training by hour 12.
  • Explanation generation — which LLM paraphrases the rule output, and who owns the 5 % human check.
