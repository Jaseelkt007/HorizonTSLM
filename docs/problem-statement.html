Turbine Alarm Explainer

Reasoning over wind-turbine signals: a Time-Series Language Model that reads a turbine's SCADA channels and tells the operations engineer what happened, which subsystem is involved, what it cost, and whether trouble is coming.

1 · Target user

The wind-farm operations engineer at an asset owner or an independent service provider, monitoring 10–100 turbines from a control room. Their morning starts with the overnight event list: a farm of 14 turbines produces roughly 4,000 status events a year, most of them benign (low wind, battery tests, comms blips), some of them the first sign of a forced outage that costs days of production and a site visit.

Today they answer three questions by hand, scrolling 10-minute plots of 300 channels per turbine:

  • What happened here, and which subsystem is it? (triage the alarm)
  • Does it matter — how much did it cost, does it need a visit? (prioritise)
  • Is this turbine drifting toward a stop? (anticipate)

Secondary users: the site technician who gets the work order, and the asset manager who reviews lost-production reports. This is Aionic's industrials and energy lane in one asset class.

2 · Signals we have

Two native streams per turbine, exported from Greenbyte SCADA by the operator, plus static farm metadata. Everything below has been checked in the raw files.

Stream A — 10-minute SCADA telemetry (the time series)

299 columns are exported; only ~70 are actually populated (the rest are empty channels for this turbine type). The model input is a curated set of ~16 base signals, chosen because they are populated, physically meaningful, and cover the subsystems the alarms talk about:

Stream B — Status / alarm log (the language)

One row per event: Timestamp start, Timestamp end, Duration, Status, Code, Message, Comment, Service contract category, IEC category. Around 120 distinct (status, message) pairs per farm-year. Examples of real messages: Overload generator fan 2 · Brake accumulator defect · Low gearbox oil pressure · Frequency converter error · Grid loss · Tower oscillation X level 2 · Pitch measuring system 1><2 · Brake pads worn · Cable autounwind · Comm. failure FPM · Wind < start wind · Manual stop – on site. The IEC category (Full Performance, Forced outage, Scheduled Maintenance, Technical Standby, Out of Environmental Specification…) tells us whether an event was a real failure.

Static metadata

Turbine type (MM92 vs MM82), rated power (2.05 MW), rotor diameter, hub height, coordinates. This goes into the text context so the model knows which machine it is looking at.

Data facts that shape the design

Kelmarsh 2016 has temperatures / RPM / pitch only from May 2016 → we use June 2016 onward. 649 of 2,122 status rows in one turbine-year have an open end ("-") → treat as point events. "Battery test" and "Manual stop" dominate the log → excluded from the fault classes, kept as context. After filtering, ~900 real Stop/Warning events per 6 turbines per year → roughly 5,000 Kelmarsh + 8,000 Penmanshiel labelled events overall.

3 · Problems we solve

  • Separate real faults from operational noise. Thousands of events; the engineer needs the ones that indicate a component problem, with the evidence in the signals.
  • Explain, not just flag. An alarm code says what tripped; it does not say what the temperatures, power and pitch were doing in the hours before. The model narrates that.
  • Price the event. Lost production in kWh, whether the turbine is still down, whether wind is available — the engineer's basis for prioritising a site visit.
  • Anticipate. Given a quiet window, is a forced outage likely in the next hours, and in which subsystem?
  • Generalise across machines. A model trained on one farm must still read a different turbine model at another site — that is what the operator's fleet looks like.

4 · Workflow, in Aionic's four stages

Sense & encode

The system takes a 24 h window of the ~16 SCADA channels for one turbine (144 steps at 10 min), the turbine's static description, and the question. Each channel is encoded separately and described in text with its mean, std and unit — the OpenTSLM multi-series "stacking".

Reason & explain

The TSLM writes a short structured finding: which signals changed and how, what the turbine state was (producing / idle / feathered), the most plausible subsystem, and the alarm-level description — before it commits to a label.

Act & execute

One recommended action with the reason: inspect generator fan 1 before the wind returns, no action — low-wind standby, schedule hydraulic check. The engineer approves or dismisses; nothing is executed automatically.

System implications

The finding carries the price: lost production so far (kWh), whether wind is available now, and whether the same message recurred on this turbine recently — the inputs to a prioritised work list across the farm.

In one sentence

Input: 24 h of ~16 SCADA channels for one turbine + a text context + a question. Output: a short plain-language finding with evidence, subsystem, impact and action, ending in a label we can score. T2/T3 give the numbers, T1 gives the demo, T4 gives the "sense of time".

6 · Input specification

Time-series part

  • Window: 24 h = 144 steps at 10 min, ending at the event start (T1–T3) or 6 h before it (T4). 48 h for T5.
  • Channels: the ~16 base signals in section 2, fixed order, one TimeSeries each.
  • Normalisation: per-channel z-score inside the window; original mean / std / unit written into the channel's text description (OpenTSLM convention) so absolute values are not lost.
  • Missing values: forward-fill up to 3 steps, else 0 after z-scoring; windows with >20 % missing in any core channel are dropped.

Text part

  • Context: turbine id and type, rated power, month, ambient at window end, number of events of the same message on this turbine in the previous 30 days.
  • Per-channel description: "Generator bearing rear temperature, °C, 10-min, mean 39.4, std 1.1".
  • Question: one of the T1–T6 prompts, with the class list for T2–T4.
  • Not given: the alarm message, code or category — those are the answer.

Sampling and splits

  • Positive windows: one per Stop / Warning event that maps to a fault or benign-stop class (dedup events that start within 10 min of each other on the same turbine → keep the highest-severity message).
  • Normal windows: random 24 h windows with no Stop/Warning inside and none in the following 6 h, sampled to match positives 1:1.
  • Split (no leakage): Penmanshiel is the larger farm (14 turbines, ~2.3× the events), so it trains; Kelmarsh is held out entirely. Train = Penmanshiel turbines 1–12, 2016 → 2019; validation = Penmanshiel turbines 13–15, same years; test A = Penmanshiel 2020 → mid-2021, all turbines (unseen time); test B = Kelmarsh, all 6 turbines, 2016-06 → 2021 (unseen site and turbine model, MM92 vs MM82).

7 · Output specification

The model always answers in the same five-line template followed by the scored label. This is what the demo shows and what T1 is trained on:

FINDING   one sentence: what kind of event, turbine state
EVIDENCE  2–4 sentences: which channels moved, by how much, over what time, relative to wind/load
CAUSE     most plausible subsystem-level cause, hedged when the evidence is weak
IMPACT    kWh lost in the event span; wind available or not; turbine still down or not
ACTION    one recommendation, or "no action — "
Answer: <subsystem class>

How targets are produced for training (there are no technician reports in the data, so the text is derived):

  1. Label lines (CAUSE class, IMPACT numbers, Answer) come directly from the log row and the Lost Production columns.
  2. EVIDENCE sentences are generated by rules over the window: largest z-score excursions, temperature deltas vs ambient, power-vs-wind residual (using the farm's own power curve), pitch state, RPM state, step changes in grid channels. Only facts the rules verified go in.
  3. A frontier LLM paraphrases the rule output into fluent text (same recipe as OpenTSLM's HAR-CoT / Sleep-CoT), with the instruction not to add facts. A random 5 % sample is human-checked by us.
  4. Benign classes get short targets ("Low-wind standby, no fault. No action.").

10 · Not in scope, and limitations we will state

  • No remaining-useful-life or long-horizon forecasting. 10-minute data over 6 years is not enough run-to-failure history per component. (TimesFM-3 can appear as an optional power-forecast baseline, nothing more.)
  • Ground truth is the alarm log, not a technician's diagnosis. The subsystem label is what the controller reported; the true root cause may differ. We say so.
  • Many alarms have no precursor at 10-minute resolution (grid loss, comms). T4 will work for thermal and hydraulic classes and not for others; we report per class rather than hide it in an average.
  • Generated explanations are rule-derived text paraphrased by an LLM. They are faithful to the window by construction, but they are not expert-written.
  • One manufacturer. Both farms are Senvion; generalisation to Vestas / Siemens is untested.

11 · Decisions for the team

  • Split direction — proposal: train on Penmanshiel (bigger), test on Kelmarsh. Confirm once the connector shows both farms populate the same channels and message vocabulary.
  • Window length — 24 h is the proposal (144 steps). 12 h halves the compute; 48 h helps thermal drifts. Decide with the model person.
  • Channel set — the 16 in section 2 or a smaller 8 (wind, power, rotor rpm, pitch A, gen bearing rear, stator, gear oil temp, gear oil pressure)? Fewer channels = faster SoftPrompt; Flamingo does not care much.
  • T4 horizon — 6 h ahead is the proposal. Longer is more useful and harder.
  • Which of T5/T6 to attempt — only if T1–T3 are training by hour 12.
  • Explanation generation — which LLM paraphrases the rule output, and who owns the 5 % human check.
