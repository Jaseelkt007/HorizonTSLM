TL;DR
  1. The task: pick a real problem, find an open time-series dataset, load it into TimeNet with a reusable connector, fine-tune a Time-Series Language Model (OpenTSLM is the reference), beat a baseline on a leakage-free split, demo it live.
  2. The judge's steer: the use case follows the data. Aionic's own use cases are anomaly detection, drift, forecasting and plain-language explanation across healthcare, industrials, energy, finance, telecom. Any of those domains is fair game.
  3. Training model: OpenTSLM stacks several signals (e.g. HR, SpO₂, BP, RR) as separate encoded series and fuses them with text either by soft-prompting (tokens concatenated into the LLM input) or by Flamingo-style gated cross-attention. Training data is (series + prompt → answer) pairs; the LLM stays frozen, only the encoder, projector/cross-attention and LoRA adapters are trained.
  4. Also on the radar: TimeRLM (code-driven anomaly localization in very long series) and TimesFM-3 (zero-shot multivariate forecasting, non-commercial licence).
  5. Current dataset recommendation: an industrial/energy dataset with real text attached — Kelmarsh wind-farm SCADA + alarm logs (CC-BY-4.0), with Tennessee Eastman as a low-risk backup. Health/HAR/sleep/ECG are already covered by OpenTSLM and TimeNet, so they score less on novelty.

1 · The challenge, as written in the brief

Track host: Aionic Labs + ETH Agentic Systems Lab (ASL), inside the European Hackathon League event by TUM.ai and J floor. ~50 participants, up to 10 teams. Theme: “Give AI a sense of time” — turn sensor signals into an AI system that interprets what is happening, anticipates what comes next, or helps someone decide how to respond.

What we must deliver

  • Problem + data. A target user, a problem worth solving, and open-source datasets we found ourselves. Bonus: an agentic data-sourcing pipeline that searches / assesses / retrieves / validates datasets.
  • TimeNet connector. Bring the data into TimeNet: standardise signals, metadata, annotations; define the questions / labels / targets. Reusable connectors and automated ingestion pipelines are explicitly valued.
  • Train + evaluate. Train or fine-tune a TSLM. Compare with a baseline on held-out data, with splits that avoid leakage across time, subjects or devices.
  • Demo. Show how we sourced the data, real inputs, model outputs, how it helps the user. State evidence and limitations.

Submission package: working demo · code + training configuration · a checkpoint or adapter · dataset documentation · short evaluation with baseline comparison. Then a jury pitch with a live demo covering problem, approach, results, key learnings.

Judging line from the brief: “A useful problem, thoughtful data preparation, meaningful TSLM training, credible evaluation and a clear demo. We especially value well-chosen new datasets and reusable TimeNet connectors that others can build on.”

Support: TimeNet access, technical guidance, training compute.

2 · Who Aionic is and what they care about

Aionic Labs builds Time-Series Language Models (TSLMs): models that read raw sensor data, take context, and answer in natural language. OpenTSLM is their open-source model (ICML 2026, MIT licence). They are backed by SPRIND and Next Frontier AI and sell enterprise pilots. Their pitch in four verbs: detect subtle drifts and anomalies → explain in plain language → predict how the signal evolves → recommend interventions.

3 · What the judge told Jaseel

Jaseel spoke with one of the main judges of the track. Below is what was said, followed by what we could verify from the paper and docs. Where the two differ, the verified version is the one to build on.

Judge said · on choosing a dataset

Look at the Aionic use-case pages (healthcare, industrials, energy, finance, telecom). The recurring tasks are anomaly detection and analysis of the anomaly, drift, and forecasting. There is no prescribed domain — the use case follows the data we choose. Find data first, then shape the task to what Aionic's customers would actually ask.

Judge said · on how OpenTSLM is trained (healthcare example)

Take several medical time series for one patient — heart rate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, respiration. Each is a time series. You stack them and fuse them with the language model either with self-attention in the soft-prompt approach or with cross-attention in the OpenTSLM-Flamingo approach. The text side is trained with prompt / answer pairs — he phrased it as “for each time step”.

Verified from the OpenTSLM paper

Broadly correct, with one nuance. Each signal is encoded into patch tokens by a shared encoder and given its own short text description (“heart rate over 24 h at 1 Hz, mean 61, std 12”). SoftPrompt then concatenates all series tokens and text tokens into one sequence that the LLM's own self-attention processes; Flamingo instead compresses each series with a Perceiver Resampler and lets text attend to it through gated cross-attention layers. Training examples are one (all series + prompt → answer) pair per window/sample, not per time step; the loss is ordinary next-token prediction on the answer tokens. Details in section 4.

Judge said · on other models

Do not treat OpenTSLM as the only option. He pointed to TimeRLM (“Recursive Language Models Enable Precise Anomaly Localization in Long-Context Time-Series”, from the same OpenTSLM group) and TimesFM-3 (Google's zero-shot foundation model for multivariate forecasting). Section 5 covers both.

4 · How OpenTSLM works

Paper: arXiv 2510.02410, code: github.com/OpenTSLM/OpenTSLM. One idea: keep a pretrained LLM frozen, teach a small time-series encoder to produce tokens the LLM can read, and fine-tune with LoRA on (series + question → reasoning + answer) data.

4.1 Encoding a time series

InputOne signal, any length L, z-normalised. Its mean, std and time scale are written into the text description so the model still knows the absolute scale.
PatchEncoderCut into non-overlapping patches of size p → N = L / p patches. Each patch goes through a 1-D convolution to a d_enc vector, plus learnable positional encoding.
Per-series descriptionA short text such as “Lead II ECG, 10 s at 500 Hz, mean 0.02, std 0.31” is attached to each series. This is how the model knows which series is which — and it is why multiple signals can be “stacked”.

4.2 Two ways to fuse series and text

OpenTSLM-SoftPrompt

  • Patch embeddings → Transformer encoder → MLP projection into the LLM's embedding space.
  • Series tokens are concatenated into the prompt; the LLM's normal self-attention sees text and series together.
  • Trainable: encoder, projector, LoRA. LLM frozen.
  • Simple and strong on short inputs, but memory grows fast with length: ~6 GB at 10 points → ~43 GB at 10 000 points (Llama-3B), out of memory with ≥3 long series.

OpenTSLM-Flamingo

  • Patch embeddings → Perceiver Resampler → fixed number of latent tokens per series, regardless of length.
  • Gated cross-attention layers inserted every N LLM blocks: text queries attend to series keys/values; a learnable gate γ controls how much the series influences text.
  • Trainable: encoder, resampler, cross-attention layers, LoRA. LLM frozen.
  • Constant memory: ~20–22 GB (Llama-1B), ~6–7 GB (Gemma-270M). Better on long sequences.

What the LLM input looks like in SoftPrompt (each series is a “text–time-series chunk”):

pre-prompt text Z₁ series tokens“heart rate, 1 Hz, mean 61…” Z₂ series tokens“SpO₂, 1 Hz, mean 96…” Z₃ … question / post-prompt → answer

Flamingo uses the same layout but the series tokens live outside the text stream and are reached via cross-attention, with <TS> and <endofchunk> markers in the text.

4.3 Training data format and loss

One training sample = a set of series (with descriptions) + a text prompt + a target text. The target is trained with the standard autoregressive LM loss, L = −Σ log P(y_t | y_<t, prompt, series). The paper forces a structured target: a rationale first, then Answer: <label> — this is the chain-of-thought (CoT) part and is why their datasets are called HAR-CoT, Sleep-CoT, ECG-QA-CoT.

Series:  accel_x (50 Hz, 2.56 s, mean −0.02, std 0.41)
         accel_y (…)   accel_z (…)
Prompt:  "You are given three accelerometer axes from a wrist sensor.
          Which activity is the person doing? Reason step by step."
Target:  "The z-axis shows periodic peaks at ~2 Hz with large amplitude,
          consistent with foot strikes; x and y show … Answer: running"

4.4 Curriculum and hyper-parameters (what we should copy)

StageDatasetSizePurpose
1 · Encoder warm-upTSQA (synthetic multiple-choice QA)38.4 kLearn basic temporal patterns
1 · Encoder warm-upM4-Captions (ChatGPT captions of plots)80 kLearn to describe series in words
2 · Task CoTHAR-CoT (3-axis accel, 8 activities)68.5 kActivity recognition with reasoning
2 · Task CoTSleep-CoT (EEG, 5 stages)7.4 kSleep staging with reasoning
2 · Task CoTECG-QA-CoT (12-lead ECG)159.3 kClinical QA with reasoning

Backbones: Llama-3.2 1B / 3B, Gemma-3 270M / 1B. AdamW, lr 2e-4 (encoder, LoRA) and 1e-4 (projector), 10 % linear warm-up, grad-clip 1.0, early stopping with patience 5. Results: 69.9 % F1 sleep staging and 65.4 % HAR vs 9 % / 52 % for text-only fine-tuned LLMs; the 1B model beats GPT-4o on those tasks.

Implications for us

Stage 1 checkpoints exist on HuggingFace — we should start from a pretrained OpenTSLM (stage 1 or stage 2) and only run our own stage 2 on our dataset. Flamingo + Gemma-270M/1B is the safest choice for a 24 h budget and long industrial windows. Our dataset must produce (series, prompt, rationale + answer) triples; if the raw data has only labels, we generate the rationale text from the label and simple signal statistics (that is exactly what the paper did for HAR-CoT and Sleep-CoT with an LLM).

5 · Other models to know

TimeRLM — recursive language models for anomaly localisation Aug 2026

arXiv 2608.03391 · github.com/OpenTSLM/TimeRLM · CC-BY-4.0 code and data. From the same group as OpenTSLM. The problem it attacks: TSLMs like OpenTSLM-Flamingo compress a long series into a fixed number of latents, so they lose the ability to say where something happened in 100 k points.

  • Mechanism: the series is not put in the context window at all. It is an external variable; the LLM (Qwen3.5-4B + LoRA) writes Python into a sandboxed REPL to slice, filter, compute statistics, render plots (vision variant), and can spawn sub-models on segments (multi-agent variant). It answers with JSON: start/end indices, anomaly type, magnitude, affected channels.
  • Training: RL (GRPO) on a synthetic generator, AnomalyXL-Localize — contexts of 16 k–131 k points with injected level shifts, spikes, seasonality changes, variance changes, trends; five task families (localise, classify with evidence, measure magnitude, all-channels, lead-lag).
  • Results: localisation IoU 0.68 vs 0.008 for OpenTSLM-Flamingo; transfers to real ECG, sleep and software telemetry without seeing them.
  • For us: a strong option if our story is “find the anomaly in a long SCADA / telemetry window and explain it”. It is also a ready-made baseline: run it zero-shot on our test set and compare to our fine-tuned OpenTSLM.

TimesFM-3 — zero-shot multivariate forecasting 31 Aug 2026

Google Research blog · github.com/google-research/timesfm. 330 M parameters, trained on >1 T time points, first TimesFM that forecasts multiple targets jointly with past and known-future covariates, in one non-autoregressive pass. Ranks first on GIFT-Eval and FEV-Bench among pretrained foundation models.

  • Not a language model — numbers in, numbers out. It cannot explain anything.
  • Licence: weights are non-commercial, non-production (timesfm-non-commercial-license-v1.0). Fine for a hackathon, worth saying out loud in the pitch.
  • For us: the natural forecasting baseline or a tool inside our pipeline (e.g. forecast the expected signal, feed the residual/“surprise” to the TSLM as an extra series to explain). Pairing “TimesFM-3 predicts, OpenTSLM explains” is a credible architecture story.

6 · TimeNet — the data layer we must use

Docs: docs.timenet.ai · repo: github.com/OpenTSLM/TimeNet · pip install 'timenet[cli,torch]', Python ≥ 3.11. TimeNet is data only: it converts any raw dataset into one standard on-disk format (TimeF, Parquet/Zarr + manifest.json) and loads it back lazily. No training, no metrics.

Connector · our codedownload(cache_dir) fetches raw files (may use network) → convert(raw_refs) parses them into a TimeFDataset (must be offline, so it is testable with fixtures).
Builder · timenet-build build org/nameRuns download → convert → derive_schema → store into a local registry directory. Idempotent.
Client · TimeNet().load("org/name") / .load_torch(...)Loads records, series and tasks lazily; the torch loader gives item["series"] and item["tasks"] ready for a training loop.

Data model

Dataset (org/name@version) → Record (one recording; subject_ids, start_time) → TimeSeries (a typed spec with unit, signal name, regular/ordinal/irregular time axis, lazy loader) + Annotation (key/value, optional span; also used for record metadata like device or split) + Task (the training target).

Task typeTargetUse for
ClassificationTaskone label from a vocabularyfault class, activity, normal/abnormal
AnswerTaskfree text (prompt → answer, optional rationale)QA, captions, explanations — the core TSLM shape
ScalarPredictionTasknumber + unitRUL, lost production, mean HR
TemporalLocalizationTaskpoints / intervals“where is the anomaly”
ForecastingTaskfuture span of the seriesforecasting baseline vs TSLM
TSEditing / TSGeneration / TSCorrespondenceseriesprobably not for us

A whole connector is small. The shipped TSQA one is ~40 lines:

class TSQAConnector(BaseHuggingFaceConnector):
    HF_REPO = "ChengsenWang/TSQA"

    def convert(self, raw_refs):
        ds = TimeFDataset(metadata=self.metadata())
        for i, row in enumerate(raw_refs):
            ts = TimeSeries.from_values(json.loads(row["Series"]), spec=_SPEC,
                    signal="c0", time_axis=OrdinalAxis(), time_series_id=f"row-{i}")
            rec = ds.add_record(time_series=(ts,), record_id=f"row-{i}")
            rec.add_annotation(Annotation(key="split", value=row["split"], id=f"split-{i}"))
            ds.add_task(rec, AnswerTask(prompt=row["Question"], target=row["Answer"], id=f"qa-{i}"))
        return ds

CONNECTOR = TSQAConnector

Files per connector: datasets/<org>/<name>/{__init__.py, connector.py, dataset.yaml, requirements.txt}. Reusable bases exist for HuggingFace Hub, PhysioNet, EDF and Excel sources. Current catalog is only five datasets — TSQA, ECG-QA-CoT, Sleep-EDFx and two synthetic demos — which is why the brief keeps saying “new datasets and reusable connectors”.

Gap to plan for: the OpenTSLM training code does not read TimeNet directly. We need a small glue script from load_torch() items to OpenTSLM's dataset class. Ask the organisers on-site whether one already exists.

Recommendation

Primary: Kelmarsh wind-farm SCADA + status logs. The only candidate with real operator language attached to real sensors. Tasks we can build in TimeNet: ClassificationTask — which subsystem is faulting (pitch / yaw / gearbox / grid / converter) from the window before an alarm; AnswerTask — “describe what happened to turbine 3 in this window” with target built from the log message, duration and lost production; TemporalLocalizationTask — onset of the event; ScalarPredictionTask — lost production. Clean splits (turbines 1–4 train / 5–6 test, or 2016–19 / 2020). A generic “SCADA + event-log” connector is exactly the reusable thing the brief asks for. Risk: ~300 messy CSV columns — budget 2–3 h, take one or two years only.

Backup: Tennessee Eastman. Public domain, one file, 21 documented fault classes, perfect run-based splits, and the fault descriptions give a rationale target for free. Less “real”, but a working TimeNet dataset in under an hour. Doing both = two new catalog entries.

Agentic sourcing (brief bonus): a small agent that queries the HF Hub, Zenodo, UCI and PhysioNet APIs, scores hits on the criteria above, and emits a draft dataset.yaml + connector skeleton. Even if the final pick is manual, showing this in the demo ticks a jury bullet.
